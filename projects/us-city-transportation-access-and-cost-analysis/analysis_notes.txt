US CITY TRANSPORTATION ACCESS AND COST ANALYSIS

PROGRAM PURPOSE
This project compares U.S. cities using transportation access, vehicle availability, walking, income, and housing-cost indicators. It also contains an optional cab-fare checker. If historical taxi data exists for a city, a user can enter the city, trip distance, and fare and compare the quoted cost with similar historical trips.

PROGRAM LOGIC
1. Load a prepared city-level dataset.
2. Remove records missing required analytical fields.
3. Convert median monthly rent to annual rent and divide it by median household income.
4. Normalize transportation and affordability variables so they can be combined.
5. Calculate three versions of the city score using different weights.
6. Rank and classify cities.
7. Export rankings and charts.
8. If taxi trip data is available, compare a user's fare with historical trips of similar distance.

IMPORTANT VARIABLES
transit_pct: Percentage of commuters using public transportation.
walk_pct: Percentage of commuters walking to work.
no_vehicle_pct: Percentage of households with no vehicle available.
median_gross_rent: Median monthly gross rent.
median_household_income: Median annual household income.
rent_income_ratio: Annual median rent divided by median household income.
transit_score, walk_score, no_vehicle_score: Normalized 0-1 versions of transportation indicators.
affordability_score: Reverse-normalized rent-income ratio, so a higher value represents better affordability.
balanced_score: Main 0-100 composite score.
transit_heavy_score: Alternative score that gives transportation more weight.
budget_heavy_score: Alternative score that gives affordability more weight.
fare_per_mile: Historical taxi fare divided by trip distance.

METHODS USED

Feature Engineering
Why: Raw counts, dollars, and percentages do not directly answer the business question.
How: The program creates rent_income_ratio, normalized component scores, composite scores, rankings, and fare-per-mile benchmarks from the source variables.

Min-Max Normalization
Why: Transit percentages, walking percentages, vehicle availability, and housing cost burden use different ranges. They need a common scale before they can be combined.
How: Each component is transformed using (x - minimum) / (maximum - minimum), producing values between 0 and 1.

Reverse Scaling
Why: Lower housing-cost burden is desirable, while higher transit and walking values are desirable.
How: The normalized rent-income ratio is subtracted from 1 so that all component scores have the same direction: higher is better.

Composite Index / Weighted Scoring
Why: No single variable measures whether a city is practical for car-free living.
How: The balanced score combines transit (30%), no-vehicle households (25%), walking (20%), and affordability (25%). The weights are project assumptions rather than official government weights.

Sensitivity Analysis
Why: Composite rankings can change when subjective weights change.
How: The program calculates balanced, transit-heavy, and budget-heavy scenarios. Comparing the resulting ranks shows whether a city's position is robust or highly dependent on the weighting choice.

Percentile Ranking
Why: A raw score is easier to interpret when compared with the rest of the dataset.
How: pandas rank(pct=True) converts the balanced score into a relative percentile across cities.

Distance-Band Matching
Why: A short taxi ride should not be benchmarked directly against a very long ride because fixed fees and trip structure affect cost per mile.
How: The cab checker selects historical trips within 20% of the entered mileage, with a minimum half-mile tolerance.

Percentile-Based Benchmarking
Why: Taxi fares can be skewed by unusual trips, tolls, surcharges, and other extremes, so an average alone can be misleading.
How: The checker calculates the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles of fare per mile among comparable trips. A quoted rate below Q1 is labeled below the usual range, between Q1 and Q3 is within the usual range, and above Q3 is above the usual range.

Minimum Sample Rule
Why: A benchmark based on only a handful of trips would be unstable.
How: The cab checker requires at least 30 comparable historical trips. Otherwise it reports insufficient data instead of presenting a potentially misleading comparison.

SQL Aggregation and Window Functions
Why: SQL provides a reproducible way to query rankings, summarize classifications, and compare cities within states.
How: queries.sql uses GROUP BY and ranking window functions against the cleaned city-level table.

LIMITATIONS
The city score is an analytical framework, not an official livability measure. ACS commuting behavior is a proxy for transportation accessibility and does not capture every aspect of transit quality. Median rent divided by median income is a simplified affordability indicator. Taxi benchmarking depends on the availability and consistency of city-level taxi trip datasets. Fare definitions must be standardized before combining sources. Taxi results describe historical distributions and do not guarantee what a ride should legally cost. Airport fees, tolls, time-based charges, surge pricing, and local regulations can explain prices outside the usual range.
